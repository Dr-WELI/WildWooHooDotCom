Dance Your PhD 2024, Kangaroo Time wins

WildWooHoo Studio · sourced verbatim from primary press · updated 14 June 2026

On 26 February 2024, Science Magazine and the American Association for the Advancement of Science named Kangaroo Time the overall winner of the annual Dance Your PhD contest. The video, directed and performed by Dr WELI, a recent ANU PhD graduate, translates four years of behavioural ecology research on eastern grey kangaroos into a multicultural pop-cultural object.

The line that named us

Sean Cummings, writing the official announcement for Science Magazine, described the video as one that "carries meaning nonetheless in its joyful madness." On its own, that phrase would have travelled and ended there. What happened next is what made it the studio's anchor line.

The Guardian (Kelly Burke, 27 February 2024) led with it as a headline: "'Joyful madness': ANU scientist wins global prize for 'dancing his PhD' about kangaroos." A week later, on US national television, Stephanie Ruhle of MSNBC's The 11th Hour repeated the framing: "The winning video is described as joyful madness." The phrase travelled because it described something true. It is also, by coincidence, the internal brand essence of WildWooHoo Studio, the multidisciplinary creative business that has since formed around the work. Two of the world's biggest credentialing engines independently arrived at the studio's own language.

The work

Kangaroo Time is an original song, a music video, embodied choreography, and a diverse Canberra dance cast. It translates Dr WELI's PhD research on individual personality in 300+ wild eastern grey kangaroos (Macropus giganteus) at Wilsons Promontory National Park into a multicultural pop-cultural object. The cast brought together twelve dance styles from multicultural Canberra: drag, samba, salsa, ballet, Bharathanatyam, hip hop, Brazilian funk, club, and more. That casting was not decoration; it was the work. The song parallels kangaroo behavioural differences with the varying dance styles of the micro-communities living in the city. Differences lead to diversity, the music video states. It exists within any given species, it's just natural.

There was a sense of surprise and delight in it. You could tell they were having fun through the process, that it wasn't this labored, stressful experience. Alexa Meade, visual artist on the Dance Your PhD 2024 judging panel, in Science Magazine

Per Sean Cummings' reporting, the entries that year were strong enough that judges noted the second-ranked dance in the social science category "might have won the whole thing if not up against the kangaroos." Meade also offered a working definition of what the contest looks for: "This year's entries did a great job of incorporating art and science to create something greater than the sum of their parts. It has to be a blending that accentuates both."

How it landed

The work moved across multiple communication contexts: science media, mainstream news, radio, television, podcasts, social media, university communications, and cultural media. Each outlet entered through a different door. Science Magazine led with the contest. The Guardian led with the headline phrase. Smithsonian Magazine called it "a musical celebration of kangaroo behavior." NPR's All Things Considered ran a deeper piece on research, identity, and performance. ABC News ran the story across Australia. The Canberra Times covered it locally. SBS Portuguese ran it for the Australian-Brazilian diaspora. O Globo and Folha de São Paulo, Brazil's two largest dailies, ran it in Portuguese. The Eco Enthusiast podcast (Episode 35) gave it longer-form treatment. Tom Scott included it in his weekly newsletter. George Takei amplified it to a 9.3-million-follower social audience.

Per Isentia (2024) media monitoring, the project generated more than 238 media features across five continents, with an estimated potential reach exceeding 15 million people and a baseline audience of approximately 3 million. YouTube lifetime analytics, retrieved 2026, record 154,766 views, 5,978.7 hours of cumulative watch time, an average view duration of 2 minutes 19 seconds, 187,729 impressions at a 9.8% click-through rate, and a 98.2% positive sentiment ratio (3,014 likes against 55 dislikes). Off-platform referrals drove 64.5% of views, indicating that press circulation and social-media sharing, not YouTube's algorithmic surfacing, moved the audience to the work.

What it catalysed

The song and video were not the endpoint of the communication process. They functioned as catalysts for subsequent engagement: interviews, public talks, workshops, performances, science festivals, and educational events. Specific examples:

  • The opening plenary at Encontro Anual de Etologia 2024 (one of the largest ethological gatherings in Latin America).
  • The ACT National Science Week 2024 and 2025 programmes (funded by Inspiring Australia ACT).
  • A sustained series of institutional and public-facing engagements documented at drweli.com/program.
  • The launch of WildWooHoo Studio, the specialist creative studio that converts behavioural ecology and comparative cognition principles into music, audiovisual narratives, and pedagogical resources.
  • International entertainment TV (Germany's UFA Show & Factual; Canada's Productions Mustang III), the song played in nightclubs, sync-licensed for a non-scientific documentary, and performed at multiple cultural festivals locally.

What this is, and what it isn't

Reach metrics and watch-time aggregates do not directly measure comprehension or learning outcomes. The Dance Your PhD platform amplified initial visibility in ways a standalone release would not. This article documents the mechanics and circulation, augmented by platform-level engagement-quality signals, not a controlled comparison of communication outcomes. The initial goal, getting more Australians to learn about kangaroos and about diversity, was met. Everything that followed grew from that.
